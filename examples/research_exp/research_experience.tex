\subsection{Multi-layer ion trap chip and its characterization}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{minipage}{6in}
        \includegraphics[width=6 in]{figures/figure1.pdf}
        \captionsetup{width=\linewidth}
        \caption{
        (\textbf{a}) Cross-sectional view of the designed multi-layer ion trap and simulated radial field distribution (Ansys Maxwell 3D).
        (\textbf{b}) Photograph of the fabricated multi-layer ion trap chip.
        }
        \label{fig:figure1}
    \end{minipage}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=3.0em, itemsep=0.0em, topsep=-0.0cm]
    \item A multi-layer ion-trap chip composed of nine stacked layers was utilized in this work. The chip includes DC/RF layers that provide the trapping potential, shim layers for principal-axis and micromotion compensation, and spacer layers that separate the electrode layers. A shield layer is positioned on the top of the chip to collimate the ablated atomic beam. The DC electrodes are segmented to enable ion shuttling, as depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}(a, left).
    \item Simulated the designed structure with $\Omega_{\mathrm{RF}} = 2\pi\cdot 20.26~\mathrm{MHz}$ and $V_{\mathrm{RF}} = 75~\mathrm{V}$ using the Ansys Maxwell 3D electrostatic solver, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}(a, right). The simulations yield radial secular frequencies of about $3~\mathrm{MHz}$, in good agreement with the experimentally measured values.
    \item Laser-cut the alumina substrate using a picosecond laser, deposited a Ti adhesion layer and Au seed layer by sputtering, and electroplated additional Au to form the electrodes. The fabricated multi-layer chip is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}(b).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Construction of cryogenic ion trap system}
\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \begin{minipage}{6.0in}
        \includegraphics[width=6 in]{figures/figure2.pdf}
        \captionsetup{width=\linewidth}
        \caption{
        (\textbf{a}) Overall layout of the laser and optical setup for photoionization, Doppler cooling, and repumping.
        (\textbf{b}) Schematic of the cryogenic ion trap system and its main in-vacuum components.
        (\textbf{c}) Photograph of the entire cryogenic ion trap setup.
        (\textbf{d}) Fluorescence image of two trapped-ions.
        }
        \label{fig:figure2}
    \end{minipage}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=3.0em, itemsep=0.0em, topsep=-0.0 mm]
    \item Constructed the laser and optical system for photoionization (422 and 369~nm), Doppler cooling (397~nm), and repumping (866 and 854~nm) for ion trapping in the cryostat, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}(a).

    \item Constructed the cryogenic ion trap system inside the cryostat, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}(b). The main in-vacuum components are summarized as follows:
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.0em, itemsep=0.0em, topsep=0.2em]
        \item The ion trap chip was mounted and gold wire-bonded on a cryo- and vacuum-compatible PCB. Low-pass filter boards were installed on both sides of the trap and connected to the DC electrodes through ribbon cables, while the RF signal was directly fed to the trap.
        \item The ion trap assembly was enclosed in an oxygen-free high thermal conductivity (OFHC) copper chamber mounted on the cold plate of the cryostat. The chamber was cooled to 4~K by a pulse-tube cryocooler to provide cryopumping, and charcoal getters were incorporated to enhance cryosorption.
        \item A high-NA (0.38) objective lens was positioned above the trap and mounted on a cryogenic-compatible XYZ piezo translation stage for ion fluorescence imaging.
    \end{itemize}

    \item Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}(c) shows the overall cryogenic ion trap experiment, including the fluorescence imaging system with a high-gain EMCCD camera, the vacuum pumping system for maintaining the ultra-high-vacuum (UHV) environment, the helical resonator connected to the RF signal generator for stable high-Q RF delivery, the ablation loading system with a pulsed laser and calcium target, and the laser stabilization system.

    \item Implemented laser power stabilization and frequency locking using a PID control loop on an FPGA board together with a high-resolution frequency modulation system. These subsystems were integrated into the experimental system through a home-built PyQt-based UI.

    \item Demonstrated trapping of up to two ions with $(\omega_x,\omega_y)=2\pi\cdot(2.75,2.88)~\mathrm{MHz}$. A representative fluorescence image of two trapped-ions is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}(d).

    \item Presented the construction progress and preliminary experimental results of the cryogenic ion trap system at the 2024 Korean Conference on Semiconductors, where this work received an on-site outstanding poster award.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering

        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.90\linewidth]{figures/figure3.pdf}
        \captionsetup{width=0.90\linewidth}
        \caption{
            Axial displacement of a single trapped-ion used as an in-situ probe of light-induced surface potentials.
        }
        \label{fig:figure3}
    \end{minipage}%
    \hfill
    \begin{minipage}[!h]{0.45\textwidth}
        \vspace{0pt}
        \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.0em, itemsep=0.2em, topsep=0.2em, parsep=0pt]
            \item Used a single trapped-ion as a sensitive \textit{in-situ} probe of light-induced charging on the trap electrodes by monitoring its time-dependent axial displacement when a focused UV beam was applied to oxide patches on the electrode surface.
            \item Performed time-resolved measurements of the ion position during illumination and in the subsequent relaxation period, and varied beam power, exposure time, and illumination geometry to observe how these parameters qualitatively modify the charging and discharging behavior.
            \item Identified sign-changing charging and discharging effects on the oxide surface from the ion-image data, and compared these features with expectations from a trap-limited surface photovoltage (SPV) picture.
            \item Discussed how the observed photo-induced stray fields affect ion stability in our cryogenic ion-trap system.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{minipage}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Modeling of a normally-on GaN/AlGaN HEMT}

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \begin{minipage}{6in}
        \includegraphics[width=6 in]{figures/figure5.pdf}
        \captionsetup{width=\linewidth}
        \caption{
        (\textbf{a})
        Schematic of AlGaN/GaN MOS-HFET integrated with a clamp circuit,
        (\textbf{b}) TCAD (Silvaco) model structure,
        (\textbf{c}) I--V characteristics of TCAD and LTspice models
        }
        \label{fig:figure5}
    \end{minipage}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=3.0em, itemsep=0.2em, topsep=0.2em, parsep=0pt]
    \item Extended previous work in which a clamp circuit was combined with an AlGaN/GaN MOS-HFET to realize normally-off operation while targeting high switching speed and high power-conversion efficiency for high-power, high-frequency applications.
    \item Modelled an AlGaN/GaN HFET in Silvaco-ATLAS by setting parameters such as unintentional doping, polarization-induced sheet charge, and surface state density so that the simulated device reproduces the measured behaviour (2DEG concentration $4.5\times 10^{13}~\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$, threshold voltage $\sim -14~\mathrm{V}$, on-resistance $\sim 12.51~\Omega\cdot\mathrm{mm}$ at $V_{\mathrm{G}} = 0~\mathrm{V}$).
    \item Extracted channel-width-dependent I--V characteristics from DC sweep simulations and capacitance characteristics from transient simulations for use in circuit design.
    \item Converted the TCAD-based device model into a BSIM4-compatible SPICE library so that the GaN MOS-HFET can be used in circuit-level simulations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Optimization of circuit}

\begin{figure}[!h]

        (\textbf{c})
        Clamped low-side peak gate voltage and Vds as a function of clamp capacitance
        }
        \label{fig:figure6}
    \end{minipage}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=3.0em, itemsep=0.2em, topsep=0.2em, parsep=0pt]
    \item Implemented normally-off operation of the D-mode GaN MOS-HFET in LTspice by adding a clamp circuit composed of a GaN Schottky barrier diode and a multilayer metal–insulator–metal capacitor, which shifts the input gate signal by about $-18.7~\mathrm{V}$ in peak-to-peak amplitude and enables enhancement-mode operation.
    \item Chose the clamp capacitance and gate capacitance to achieve high switching speed, and verified that with a clamp capacitance of $30~\mathrm{nF}$ and a channel width of $47~\mathrm{mm}$ the device supports switching frequencies up to $35~\mathrm{MHz}$.
\end{itemize}
